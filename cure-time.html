Cure time estimator

Your data sheet promises "full cure in 24 hours" — at the lab temperature printed beside it, usually about 23 °C. A shop in October is not that lab. Cure speed is chemistry, and chemistry runs on temperature: as a rule of thumb, the reaction rate doubles for every 10 °C warmer and halves for every 10 °C colder.1 This estimator rescales any cure milestone from your data sheet — working time, tack-free, demold, full cure — to the temperature on your bench, and says plainly when the estimate is stretched thin.

Notes

  1. The doubling rule (Q10 ≈ 2) is a rule of thumb, not a law. Real epoxy systems follow Arrhenius kinetics, and the effective doubling interval varies by chemistry — roughly 8–13 °C in practice. Close to the data-sheet temperature the estimate is good. Far from it, treat the number as a planning range and let the piece itself tell you: a thumbnail that leaves no mark beats a clock.
  2. Data sheets usually state cure times at 21–25 °C (70–77 °F). If yours states a different reference temperature, enter that instead of the 23 °C prefill — the estimate is only as good as the baseline it scales from.
  3. The temperature that matters is the resin's and the workpiece's, and a cold slab in a warm room cures at slab temperature for the first hours. Mass matters too: a thick pour generates its own heat and its core runs warmer than the room, so deep pieces can reach milestones early in the middle while the surface lags. Thin coats track room temperature closely.
  4. Below about 15 °C many systems cure very slowly, and some amine-cured epoxies develop blush or never reach full properties no matter how long you wait. Every product has a minimum application temperature on its data sheet, and that number governs — warm the room, the resin bottles, and the workpiece rather than extending the clock.

Safety

  • Work in a ventilated space. Wear an organic-vapour respirator, nitrile gloves, and eye protection. Uncured epoxy is a skin sensitiser, and an allergy builds with every exposure.
  • Don't force a faster cure with a heat gun or torch on a curing piece: local overheating can bubble, yellow, or crack the resin, and a thick pour can run away. Gentle, even room warmth is the only safe accelerator.
  • Not food-safe unless the specific product is certified for food contact and fully cured to its data sheet — full cure at your temperature, not the lab's.
